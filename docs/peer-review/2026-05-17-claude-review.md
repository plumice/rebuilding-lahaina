# Peer Review: "Rebuilding Post Disasters: Lahaina — A Multi-Scalar Recovery Framework for Post-Disaster Reconstruction"

**Author:** Akhil Singh (M.Arch 2025, Tulane University School of Architecture)
**Reviewer:** Claude (Opus 4.7), structural/editorial peer review for publication preparation
**Date:** 17 May 2026
**Manuscript:** 37,914 words, 2,278 lines (`thesis_source.txt`)
**Recommendation:** Major revision required before publication; the underlying work is strong enough to publish, but not in current form and not as a single article.

---

## 1. Summary

The thesis proposes a multi-scalar recovery framework for post-wildfire Lahaina organized around four buffer zones (coastal, riparian, peri-urban, hinterland), three interlocking infrastructural systems (water, mobility, community hubs), and a demonstration building (a primary-school/community-hub modeled on the "mat building" typology of van Eyck, Correa, and Hertzberger, with structural references to the Hawaiian *hale*). The central argument: the August 8, 2023 fire was the surfacing of decades of misalignment between Lahaina's plantation/tourism-era built environment and its ahupuaʻa-organized watershed, and recovery must therefore re-integrate water, land tenure, mobility, and cultural memory at multiple scales simultaneously, or it will reproduce the conditions that produced the disaster. The author offers this as a transferable methodology, not a Lahaina-only prescription.

## 2. Originality & Contribution — **Adequate, with significant overreach**

**What is genuinely new:**
- The specific synthesis of Alday/Jover's "daily resilience" + Beamer's ahupuaʻa logic + Hawaiian *hale* structural rules applied through a single mat-building module at building scale (Sec. 3D/3E, ~lines 1054–1271). This trinity does not exist in the literature this reviewer can identify.
- The detailed water-systems table (lines 492–632) mapping eleven plantation-era diversions to their post-1999 condition, drawing on WSP USA's 2024 reconnaissance survey alongside CWRM data. This is original synthetic work and the strongest empirical contribution.
- The photo-telemetric Front Street density study using historical Google Street View (2012/2015/2019/2022) (lines 286–289, 841–846). The method is rudimentary but the comparative time-series application to post-disaster planning is novel.

**Where the contribution claim overreaches:**
- The "transferable methodology" framing in Sec. 4B (line 1315–1321) is asserted, not demonstrated. The thesis tests the framework on exactly one site, with one alternate program (the fire station competition entry), both in Lahaina. Claiming transferability without a second site application is rhetoric, not contribution. **Recommendation:** either soften to "potentially transferable" with stated boundary conditions, or add a brief speculative second-site test (Paradise CA, Lytton BC) showing how the buffer logic would re-form.
- The four-buffer framework (coastal/riparian/peri-urban/hinterland) is not new. Greater New Orleans Urban Water Plan, the Dutch *Room for the River* program, BIG's *Big U*, and FEMA's nature-based solutions guidance all use multi-edge buffer logics. The thesis cites Waggonner & Ball but does not differentiate its framework from theirs. **Recommendation:** add a one-paragraph "what is new about these buffers" passage early in Sec. 3C explicitly distinguishing the framework from the prior art it builds on.

**Where the contribution underreaches:**
- The hale–mat synthesis at Sec. 3E (lines 1053–1271) is more original than the buffer system, but it is presented modestly as a "demonstration." It deserves to be the lead contribution of at least one of the spin-off papers. The author appears not to recognize that fusing HAR §15-110 structural rules with the Smithson mat lineage is a publishable design-research contribution in its own right.

**Verdict:** Adequate. The work has 2–3 publishable contributions but is currently presenting them as one inflated claim.

## 3. Research Question & Hypothesis — **Needs Work**

The five research questions (lines 37–41) are well-motivated but uneven in answerability:

- Q1 (systemic vulnerabilities) — answerable; thesis answers it well.
- Q2 (cultural identity as operational, not preserved) — answerable; the Mokuʻula-as-water-infrastructure move (line 284) is the clean answer.
- Q3 (NOLA/Kobe lessons) — answerable but the thesis treats these precedents thinly; see §5 below.
- Q4 (preventing displacement) — the author admits this "has no easy answer, but it cannot be avoided" (line 40). This is honest but it also signals that the question, as posed, is unfalsifiable within the scope of a design thesis. The work proposes mechanisms (CLT, inclusionary zoning, right-of-first-refusal) but cannot evaluate them. **Recommendation:** reframe Q4 as "what *spatial and tenurial design parameters* would make anti-displacement instruments effective?" — a question the thesis can actually answer.
- Q5 (community participation methods) — the thesis answers this weakly (see §7 below).

The hypothesis (lines 43) is a long sentence containing four discrete claims:
1. Integrated water + resilient housing form the foundation of long-term recovery.
2. Cultural identity strengthens legitimacy when operational.
3. Community-driven processes outperform agency-driven ones over time.
4. Iterative multi-scalar methodology enhances adaptability.

None of these is falsifiable as stated. Claim 3 is partially testable against precedent (Broadmoor vs. Lower Ninth), and the thesis does test it. Claims 1, 2, and 4 are framework assertions, not hypotheses. **Recommendation:** for the journal version, reduce the hypothesis to a single falsifiable claim (e.g., "Spatially-integrated buffer + node systems produce reconstruction proposals more resilient to disaster gentrification than agency-led permitting reforms alone, when measured by [X criterion].") Reserve the others as design principles, not hypotheses.

## 4. Methodology — **Needs Work**

The multi-scalar logic (lines 324–366) is clearly articulated and the four-scale nesting (regional/urban/neighborhood/site) is defensible. However, several specific methodological omissions would draw immediate reviewer fire:

**Major gaps:**
- **No GIS weighting matrix.** Section 3A says layers were "compiled" and "synthesized" into a "composite spatial picture" (line 322), but there is no specification of how layers were weighted in the composite. When five overlay layers produce a "redensification zone," reviewers will demand to know whether SLR exposure was weighted equally to fuel load and tenure fragmentation, or whether one dominated. **Recommendation:** add Sec. 3A.4 with a weighting matrix and a sensitivity test showing how the redensification zone shifts under different weightings.
- **No reproducibility statement.** The reader cannot replicate the analysis from what is documented. USGS DEM, Rainfall Atlas of Hawaiʻi, FEMA damage assessments are named, but versions, dates, projection systems, and processing software (ArcGIS Pro? QGIS? Rhino Grasshopper?) are not. **Recommendation:** add a one-page Data & Methods appendix specifying datasets (with URLs and access dates), software, GIS projection (likely UTM Zone 4N or NAD83 HARN Hawaii Zone 2), and processing steps.
- **The photo-telemetric method is under-described.** Sec. 2B says "isolated pedestrian presence within sequential photographs, marking location and relative density to identify crux points" (line 288). How? Manual count? Computer vision? What was the sample interval along Front Street? How was "density" quantified — pedestrians per frame, per linear foot? Without this, the "three concentrations emerged consistently" finding is anecdote. **Recommendation:** add a Methods box specifying sample geometry, counting protocol, inter-rater reliability if any.
- **Iterative design phases (diagnosis/projection/implementation/synthesis, lines 367–383) are asserted as method but not documented as practice.** The reader sees the final proposal, not the iterations. **Recommendation:** show one intervention (e.g., the boulevard placement) through its iterations with the disqualifying constraint at each rejection. This is the standard for design-research methodology in JAE and Built Environment.

**Minor:** The author uses first-person throughout Sec. 3A ("I conducted," "I compiled"). For JAE this is acceptable. For *Disasters* or *Built Environment* this should be converted to third-person passive or "this study" voice. See §8.

**Verdict:** The method is real and defensible but under-documented. Two added pages would close most reviewer questions.

## 5. Literature Engagement — **Needs Work**

The author engages substantively with several core thinkers (Vale & Campanella, Gotham, Johnson & Olshansky, Alday & Jover, Alday & Gupta, Beamer, Kameʻeleihiwa, Aldrich is cited but barely engaged) and the framing of disaster recovery as political-economic process is sound. However, there are significant omissions and shallow treatments.

**Strong engagement:**
- Alday/Jover (lines 298–302) — three specific transferable claims pulled out and applied.
- Alday/Gupta Yamuna (lines 300–302) — the "Urban Layers" methodology is genuinely used as method foundation.
- Beamer's ahupuaʻa (lines 303, 1984–1985) — substantive use, not name-dropping.

**Weak engagement (named but not used):**
- **Daniel Aldrich** (line 305) — cited once for the social-capital-predicts-recovery finding, never used to interpret Lahaina's mutual aid networks. Lahaina Strong's protest camp and Ka Koʻo Maui's land-trust work are exactly Aldrich's bonding/bridging/linking capital distinction, but the thesis doesn't apply the framework. **Recommendation:** add a short subsection in Sec. 2C or 3D using Aldrich's three capital types to classify the community organizations.
- **Naomi Klein** (line 313) — cited for "disaster capitalism" but treated as a label, not engaged. The "shock doctrine" framework would substantially strengthen the disaster-gentrification critique. The author seems to soften it ("polemical, but the mechanism is empirical").
- **Kenneth Frampton** is in the bibliography (line 1468, citing Frampton on Correa) but Critical Regionalism is never invoked, despite the thesis being a Critical Regionalist project in every meaningful sense (the *hale*-mat synthesis IS Critical Regionalism applied to disaster recovery). **Recommendation:** add a short Critical Regionalism passage in Sec. 2C — this is low effort, high return for academic legitimacy.

**Notable omissions:**
- **Davis/Kelman/Wisner** ("disasters as social construct" / vulnerability paradigm) — the entire "vulnerabilities accumulated over decades" framing comes from this tradition (Wisner et al., *At Risk*, 2004) but is uncited. This is a serious omission for any *Disasters* journal submission. **Recommendation:** add Wisner, Blaikie, Cannon, Davis, *At Risk: Natural Hazards, People's Vulnerability and Disasters*, 2nd ed. (Routledge, 2004) to the bibliography and cite at line 224 ("Every latent vulnerability surfaced simultaneously").
- **Anthony Oliver-Smith** on disaster anthropology and forced resettlement — directly relevant to the "right to return" framing.
- **Mike Davis,** *Ecology of Fear* (1998) — the foundational WUI text; conspicuously absent from a wildfire-recovery thesis.
- **Trisha Kehaulani Watson** and other Native Hawaiian scholars on contemporary water-rights cases (the *In re ʻIao Ground Water Management Area* litigation, the Kauaʻula stream restoration decisions) — the thesis references CWRM IIFS decisions in tables but does not engage the Native Hawaiian legal scholarship around them.
- **Kapā Oliveira, Noenoe Silva, Jonathan Osorio** — for indigenous methodologies and sovereignty framing. If the thesis is going to be published in journals read by Native Hawaiian scholars (and it should be), this corpus matters.
- **Charles Correa's own writing** (*A Place in the Shade*) — cited as design precedent but his theoretical writing on tropical urbanism is uncited and would directly support the climate-responsive architecture claims.
- **Aldo van Eyck's** "Steps Toward a Configurative Discipline" IS in the bibliography (line 1502) but is not cited or quoted in the mat-typology section where it is foundational. The Smithsons' Berlin Hauptstadt and Team 10 framing are missing — necessary if claiming the mat lineage seriously.

**Bibliography depth issue:** the bibliography is broad but shallow — many entries are cited once or only in endnotes. **Recommendation:** the published version should cut citations not actually used and add 6–10 substantive ones from the omissions above.

**Verdict:** Adequate range, but Wisner, Davis (Mike), and Aldrich (used properly) are publication-blockers if missing.

## 6. Argument & Evidence — **Adequate, uneven across chapters**

**Chapter 2 (Overview / Literature)**

- Strongest: the historical arc from pre-contact through Māhele to plantation to tourism (lines 191–214). Tight, well-documented, makes the structural-vulnerability case efficiently.
- Weakest: Sec. 2B "Site Visit" (line 233–235) is literally empty — just three line breaks. This is a placeholder that survived into the published file and must be either deleted or filled.

**Chapter 3 (Methodology + Analysis)**

- Strongest arguments:
  - The water-systems table (lines 474–490) — the deficit math (4.26 MGD supply vs. 6.04 MGD projected demand = -1.78 MGD) makes the housing-blocked-by-water claim concrete and unanswerable.
  - The Mokuʻula-as-water-infrastructure reframing (line 284) — original, defensible, elegant.
  - The Broadmoor sequencing argument (school → family → infrastructure → commerce, line 295) — applied to justify the school-as-first-hub move. Logically tight.

- Weakest arguments:
  - The 84 acres × 26 units/acre = 2,200-unit arithmetic (lines 649, 1937) is presented as if it solves displacement. It does not. The thesis never addresses *who* gets these units, *at what rents*, *under what tenure*. "Inclusionary zoning requiring 20% affordable units" (line 651) is stated as a principle but the spatial framework does not actually encode it — the redensification diagrams show housing typologies, not tenure or AMI distribution. **Recommendation:** either add a tenure/affordability overlay to the redensification mapping, or acknowledge the gap between the spatial proposal and the policy mechanism.
  - "Every place has its own indigenous knowledge systems" (line 1317, Sec. 4B) — true but vague. As stated, this risks reading as universalizing indigenous knowledge into a generic recovery-design input.
  - The mobility section (3D-b, lines 829–870) contains numerous unsupported assertions about pre-fire conditions ("a mobility system that is designed just for daily convenience can't really accommodate it when emergency mass movement" — line 839). These need source citations (MPD incident reports, NTSB-equivalent state report on the fire, the State AG's Comprehensive Report).

- **Logical leaps:**
  - The claim that the 26-foot module's 12-26% demand offset from rainwater harvesting "demonstrates how public architecture can contribute to distributed water infrastructure" (line 1227) — this is true but the framing implies replicability. 12% offset at a 57,460 sq ft school does not scale linearly to 2,200 small-footprint residential units. Needs a back-of-envelope check at residential roof scale.
  - "Insurance companies have paid over $2 billion in claims" (line 256) and "$4 billion settlement" (line 256) — sourced to FRONTLINE and 2024 settlement documents, but the relationship of these to the 1,500-units permitting backlog is asserted, not shown.

- **Numeric claims needing tighter sourcing:**
  - "More than 80 percent of residents were renters" / "83% renter-occupied" (multiple appearances, e.g., lines 30, 232, 314, 651, 874, 1941) — the ACS 2018–2022 5-year estimate is cited at endnote 158 (line 1941). Standardize the figure (80% vs. 83%) throughout and cite at first use, not 30+ lines later.
  - "Over 1,500 families have left Hawaiʻi" (line 237) vs. "1,500–1,800 families had permanently relocated" (endnote 159) — reconcile.

**Chapter 4 (Results & Contribution)**

- Sec. 4A "Results and Discussion" is one paragraph (line 1312–1313) and is functionally empty. There is no "results" section. The work has been argued through Ch. 3; Ch. 4 should explicitly enumerate the framework's outputs against the five research questions and the three evaluation criteria the author set up in Sec. 3A. **This is a major structural problem.**
- Sec. 4B (lines 1314–1322) is the real conclusion and is reasonably good, though it overclaims (see §2).

**Verdict:** The diagnostic arguments are strong; the proposal arguments are weaker; the results chapter is missing.

## 7. Cultural & Ethical Considerations — **Needs Work; flag for Native Hawaiian reviewer**

This is the most sensitive dimension of the work and where the thesis is most exposed.

**What works:**
- The acknowledgement of being a non-Hawaiian working on Native Hawaiian land is implicit in the dedication (lines 13–20) and the use of "mahalo" to the Maui community (line 25). It is not made explicit in the methodology, however.
- The Hawaiian glossary (lines 148–190) is real — these terms are *used* throughout the document, not deposited and forgotten. Diacriticals (ʻokina and kahakō) are consistently present.
- The Beamer / Kameʻeleihiwa engagement is substantive (see §5).
- The reframing of Mokuʻula as operational water infrastructure rather than museum-piece (line 284) shows an attempt to honor the site's living function rather than freezing it.

**Concerns:**

- **Author positionality is never explicitly addressed in the text.** The author is an Indian-American (per project context, originally Houston/NYC) producing a recovery framework for Native Hawaiian land. This requires an explicit positionality statement, ideally in the methodology or in a new preface section. Standard for any post-disaster work on indigenous land; required if submitting to *Disasters*, *Environment and Urbanization*, or any journal with indigenous-studies review pool. **Recommendation:** add a 150–300 word positionality paragraph before Sec. 3A.

- **Consultation evidence is thin.** Sec. 3A "Stakeholder Collaboration and Methodology in Action" (lines 392–395) is the only consultation passage. It says: "I met with people—residents, community organizers, folks working in firms trying to help" and "I attended workshops." This is too informal to constitute community-based research. The bibliography lists "Singh, Akhil. Community Workshop Attendance and Resident Conversations. 2024" (line 1596) as if it were a citable source — it is not. **Recommendation:** either (a) document the consultation more rigorously (which organizations, what was discussed, did anyone review the framework, did the author present back to community?), or (b) explicitly frame the work as a designer's-eye analysis informed by but not co-produced with the community, and remove implied community endorsement.

- **The displacement framing is ethically sound in intent but architecturally one-sided.** The framework treats "right to return" as the design parameter (good) but does not engage seriously with the harder question: what if existing fragmented land tenure cannot be consolidated for redensification without itself producing displacement (eminent domain, forced buyouts)? The thesis assumes a CLT-led acquisition pathway but does not address what happens to private property owners (many of them Native Hawaiian kuleana holders) whose lots fall within the proposed boulevard or buffer alignments. **Recommendation:** add a short section on the tenure-displacement tension within the framework itself.

- **Hawaiian language usage** appears largely correct (ʻāina, ahupuaʻa, mauka/makai, kuleana, kānaka maoli, etc.) but I am not qualified to confirm correctness of all terms or to assess whether the *use* of Hawaiian terms by a non-Hawaiian author respects appropriate protocol. The thesis treats Hawaiian terms as operational vocabulary, which Beamer and others endorse, but the author should be reviewed by a Native Hawaiian scholar before publication. Specific terms to verify:
  - "lā hainā, 'cruel sun'" (line 193) — the standard etymology debate is more contested than the thesis suggests.
  - "lele" as traditional name for Lahaina (line 175) — sourcing.
  - Diacritical consistency throughout — check that all instances of e.g. "Kaʻanapali" carry the ʻokina (some appear without it, lines 695, 693).

- **Lack of engagement with the contemporary Kanaka Maoli sovereignty movement.** The thesis treats Hawaii statehood and Hawaiian Kingdom history as historical context, not as contested present-day politics. Ka Lāhui Hawaiʻi, the Independence movement, and the 2017–2019 Maunakea protests are absent. For a thesis arguing recovery should center kānaka maoli, this is a striking omission.

**Verdict:** Needs work, especially explicit positionality and stronger consultation documentation. **Mandatory:** Native Hawaiian scholar reviewer before publication.

## 8. Writing Quality — **Needs Work**

This is the area requiring the most labor for journal publication.

**General observations:**
- Academic register is inconsistent. The Ch. 1 abstract and Ch. 3A methodology read as polished academic prose. Sections of 3D (e.g., the water and mobility before/after/proposed structure, lines 745–870) drop into a casual, conversational register with hallmarks of underedited dictation or AI-assisted drafting: "If you think about it," "It's set up to," "you can see this in," "another thing is that," "the thing is," "etc." appearing dozens of times.
- Examples of register slippage:
  - "If you think about it, this setup helps in two big ways." (line 802)
  - "It’s set up to gravity-fed supply, so it independent of electrical pumping to work." (line 777) — also ungrammatical
  - "You could really see that Lahaina had a big issue because they depended on a single-source distribution network" (line 750)
  - "Imagine a network where there are community anchors like primary schools…" (line 931)
- Grammatical errors are frequent in Sec. 3D, including dropped articles ("They had minimal backup capacity. There was also a lack…"), tense slips ("the buffer absorbs… absorb"), and dropped verbs ("This is allowing deployment for irrigation…", line 777).
- Many tables include text fragments where definitions appear to have been truncated mid-edit (e.g., glossary entries lines 49–76 vary in punctuation and capitalization).
- Some figure callouts reference figures that are not in the final figure list (e.g., "Figure 3.23" referenced at line 369 is "Diagnostic Overlay" but the figure list at line 1358 has Figure 3.13 as "Diagnostic Overlay"). The figure numbering is inconsistent between callouts and the figure registry — a publication-blocking issue.
- First-person ("I conducted," "I went to Hawaiʻi") in Sec. 3A is acceptable for JAE but inappropriate for *Built Environment* or *Disasters*. Plan for two voicings depending on target journal.
- Repetition: the 80% renter statistic, the 6,000–7,000 displaced figure, and the "Pioneer Mill closure 1999" point each appear five-plus times across chapters. Tolerable in a thesis, intolerable in an article.
- Structural problems:
  - Sec. 2B has subsection numbering "1a_1, 1a_2, 1a_3..." then jumps to "1b" then to "1c" with no clear hierarchy.
  - Sec. 3D-c "Densification" subsections labeled "1, 2, 3" while other systems use "Before / After / Proposed" — standardize.
  - Sec. 3D-d "Network of Nodes" begins with subsection "4. Node Locations" (line 930). There is no 1, 2, or 3.
  - Endnote numbering breaks: notes jump from 281 to 294 (lines 2186–2188).
- Empty placeholders: "Site Visit" (line 233), "F. Systems Synthesis" (line 955), Figure callouts with "(WILL BE UPDATED)" (line 957). These must be resolved.

**Recommendation:** A full copyedit pass is required before any journal submission. Estimated 25–40 hours of editorial labor.

**Verdict:** Needs Work. Major Issue if submitting in current state.

**Strengths:**

Reissberg, *Managing Natural Catastrophes* (line 1483) — no in-text reference. Burney, *Back to the Future in the Caves of Kauaʻi* (line 1465) — no in-text reference. Walker & Salt, *Resilience Thinking* (line 1489) — no in-text reference. These should either be cited or removed.
- **Endnote duplication.** Notes 1, 2 etc. (lines 1626–1629) present the citation twice in adjacent lines — looks like a draft artifact where a short-form and long-form citation system was never reconciled.
- **Endnote 144 is incorrect.** Line 1912 cites "Hawaiʻi Sea Level Rise Report, Chapter 3.13" but the in-text reference at the *Hertzberger* passage (line 1050) should be a citation to Hertzberger's writing, not the SLR report. Endnote numbering and in-text superscripts are not aligned. This will be caught in copy-edit but signals broader citation-system breakdown.
- **Missing canonical citations** (see §5): Wisner et al., Mike Davis, Oliver-Smith, additional Native Hawaiian scholars.
- **Inconsistent date citations.** Endnote 9 cites Johnson and Olshansky 2010 (line 1643); the bibliography lists it as 2017 (line 1474). The Lincoln Institute book is 2017 (the 2010 date appears to be confused with an earlier Lincoln working paper). Verify.
- **URL handling.** A few URLs are referenced but not provided (e.g., "Mokuʻula ArcGIS StoryMap, https://storymaps.arcgis.com" line 1554 — the bare domain is not a citable URL). Add the full StoryMap URLs and access dates.

The PBS documentary is a fine source but for the death toll, the primary source should be the State of Hawaii Department of Health, Maui Police Department, or the State AG's Comprehensive Report — all of which are in the bibliography but not used at this point.

**Verdict:** Bibliography is adequate in scope but needs aggressive cleanup before publication.

## 10. Publication Strategy

This thesis is 38K words. No journal will accept it as a single article. Recommend chunking into 3 papers with the website (rebuildinglahaina.org) continuing to serve as the unified long-form reference.

### Paper 1 — Methods / Diagnostic
**Target:** *Journal of the American Planning Association* OR *Built Environment* (Special Issue on disaster recovery)
**Working title:** *"Multi-Scalar Diagnostic Mapping for Post-Wildfire Recovery: A Case from Lahaina, Maui"*
**Word target:** 8,000–10,000
**Contents:** Sec. 2B subject overview (condensed), Sec. 3A methodology (expanded with the weighting matrix and Methods box recommended in §4), Sec. 3B regional analysis, Sec. 3C buffer principles, water system table.
**Justification:** This is the strongest part of the work and is the most directly transferable. It positions the author as a methodologist.
**Required changes:** Add Data & Methods appendix; add weighting matrix; remove design proposal sections; tighten literature engagement (especially Wisner, Aldrich, Davis); positionality statement.

### Paper 2 — Architectural Design Research
**Target:** *Journal of Architectural Education* OR *arq: Architectural Research Quarterly*
**Working title:** *"The Mat Building Returns: Hale Construction, Climatic Modularity, and a School for Post-Fire Lahaina"*
**Word target:** 6,000–8,000 plus design drawings
**Contents:** Sec. 3E (Primary School), mat typology lineage from Sec. 3D, hale-structural translation (HAR §15-110), module performance tables, all renders/drawings.
**Justification:** This is the most genuinely original synthesis in the work. JAE specifically welcomes design-research that translates vernacular precedent through contemporary structural systems.
**Required changes:** Expand Critical Regionalism framing (Frampton); add van Eyck/Smithson primary-source engagement; cut down on Lahaina urban context (reference the Paper 1 / website); explicit positionality.

### Paper 3 — Political / Ethical
**Target:** *Disasters* (Wiley) OR *Environment and Urbanization* (SAGE/IIED)
**Working title:** *"Tenure, Water Rights, and the Limits of Spatial Recovery Frameworks: Lessons from Lahaina"*
**Word target:** 7,000–9,000
**Contents:** Anti-displacement mechanisms from Sec. 1F and 3C principles; CLT discussion; Lahaina Strong / Ka Koʻo Maui political organizing; honest discussion of the framework's limits acknowledged in Sec. 4B (lines 1320); engagement with Aldrich, Wisner, Klein.
**Justification:** *Disasters* and *E&U* explicitly value the political-ethical critique that the thesis only partially makes. This paper would force the author to make the critique fully.
**Required changes:** This is the paper that needs the most new writing. Approximately 40% would be new material critically reflecting on the framework's enabling conditions (regulatory will, community organizing capacity, water-rights politics).

### Alternative venues
- *Landscape Journal* (LAF) — for a fourth paper specifically on the four-buffer framework as landscape architecture.
- *Hulili: Multidisciplinary Research on Hawaiian Well-Being* — for a Hawaiian-studies-oriented version of Paper 3, ideally co-authored with a Native Hawaiian collaborator.
